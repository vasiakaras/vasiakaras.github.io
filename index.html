I am a Ph.D. candidate in Clinical Psychology at the University of British Columbia, supervised by Dr. Amori Yee Mikami. I study how people use the internet and how it shapes their mental health, relationships, and safety. My dissertation looks at how people search for information about ADHD online, how that content affects what they believe and do, and how clinicians can respond to it.

As a therapist, I take a collaborative, warm, and evidence-based approach. I combine cognitive-behavioural techniques with compassion-focused strategies so that treatment fits the person in front of me. I have trained in CBT, DBT, and psychodynamic therapy, and have worked with individuals, couples, and groups on anxiety, depression, OCD, trauma, and relationship and sexual health concerns.

I grew up in Greece, and I pay close attention to how culture shapes the way people understand themselves, their relationships, and their sexuality. Before UBC, I completed an M.A. in Psychology (forensic stream) and undergraduate degrees in Psychology and in Neuroscience & Mental Health at Carleton University.

Research

My work falls under what I call digital citizenship, meaning how people interact with the online world and what that does to their lives. I have three lines of research:

  • Mental health information online. How people find and evaluate ADHD content on platforms like TikTok, how it shapes self-perception and attitudes toward treatment, and how social media can be used in ways that support wellbeing.
  • Online sexual communication and consent. How consent is communicated, assumed, or ignored in sexting and other digital sexual encounters.
  • Technology-facilitated sexual violence. Non-consensual sharing of intimate images, cyberflashing, and AI-generated (deepfake) sexual content: why people do it, how survivors are affected, and where the law falls short.
